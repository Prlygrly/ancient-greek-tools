Diacritical Marks

The little commas, slashes, and dots above (and below) the letters

Ancient Greek texts use several small marks above and below vowels to indicate breathing, pitch accent, and other features. You’ll encounter them immediately when reading, so it’s worth recognizing them early.

Breathing Marks

Every word beginning with a vowel (or ρ) gets one of these.

ἀ
Smooth Breathing
ψιλὴ προσῳδία
Looks like a little ʾ (curved right). Means no “h” sound.
ἐν = “en” (in)
Ἁ
Rough Breathing
δασεῖα προσῳδία
Looks like a little ʿ (curved left). Means add an “h” sound.
ὑπό = “hypo” (under)

Accent Marks

ά
Acute Accent
ὀξεῖα
Slash rising right: ´. Pitch rises on this syllable.
λόγος = “lógos” (word)
ὰ
Grave Accent
βαρεῖα
Slash falling right: `. Replaces acute on last syllable in connected speech — pitch stays level.
καλὸς καὶ ἀγαθός
ᾶ
Circumflex
περισπωμένη
Small arc: ̂. Pitch rises then falls on one syllable. Only on long vowels/diphthongs.
μᾶλλον = “mâllon” (more)

Other Marks

ᾳ
Iota Subscript
ὑπογεγραμμένη
Tiny ι written under α, η, or ω. A silent remnant of an old diphthong. In uppercase, written next to the letter instead (ΑΙ, ΗΙ, ΩΙ).
τῇ θεᾷ = “to the goddess” (dative)
ϊ
Diaeresis
διαίρεσις
Two dots ¨ over ι or υ. Signals this vowel is separate from the preceding vowel, not a diphthong.
προϊέναι = “pro-ienai” (to go forward)
ἄ
Combinations
Breathing + accent together
Breathing and accent marks frequently stack on the same vowel. All six combos:
ἄ smooth+acute  ἂ smooth+grave  ἆ smooth+circ.
Ἁ rough+acute  Ἂ rough+grave  Ἆ rough+circ.

Don’t panic. The breathing marks and acute accent account for the vast majority of what you’ll see. The rest becomes second nature after a few weeks of reading.
